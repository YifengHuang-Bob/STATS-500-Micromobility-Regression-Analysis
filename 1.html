1. Introduction

Urban micromobility systems, such as shared scooters and bicycles, have become an integral component of transportation networks in many U.S. cities. In Austin, shared micromobility provides an accessible and flexible travel option for short urban trips and serves as a complement to public transit. Understanding how individuals use these systems is important for city planners, policymakers, and operators, as trip-level patterns can inform infrastructure planning, fleet management, and operational decision-making.

In this project, we analyze all shared micromobility trips recorded in Austin during the year 2022. These data contain detailed trip-level information, including trip duration, distance traveled, time of day, day of week, month, vehicle type, and spatial indicators of trip origin. With more than 850,000 trips, the dataset provides a rich opportunity to study behavioral patterns and identify factors associated with variation in usage.

Our analysis focuses on two related goals. First, we examine which temporal, spatial, and vehicle-related factors are associated with trip duration after adjusting for trip distance. Second, we distinguish short from long trips by modeling the probability that a trip exceeds ten minutes. Together, these analyses provide insight into how micromobility systems are used and which characteristics of a trip or rider environment most strongly influence trip length.

2.1 Data Cleaning

## [1] 294410     20
## [1] 294309      9

To ensure the quality and consistency of the analytic dataset, several preprocessing steps were applied prior to conducting the statistical analyses. First, trips with non–positive duration or non–positive distance were removed, as these values indicate incomplete or erroneous records. Second, several categorical variables contained the string “None’’ or empty entries; these values were converted to missing and subsequently removed if they occurred in variables required for modeling. In particular, we retained observations only if trip duration, trip distance, vehicle type, hour of day, day of week, month, and the council district of trip origin were all observed.

After filtering missing or invalid entries, we constructed two additional variables used in later analyses. A binary indicator for long trips was defined as 1 if the trip duration exceeded 600 seconds (10 minutes) and 0 otherwise. This classification aligns with operational considerations that distinguish short urban trips from longer rides. Because the distribution of trip duration is strongly right–skewed, we also created a log–transformed duration variable to stabilize variance and improve model fit in the linear regression analysis.

Starting from the original 2022 subset of the data, which contains 858,100 trips and 18 variables, these cleaning steps produce a cleaned working dataset with 294,410 observations and 20 variables (including the derived long–trip indicator and log–transformed duration). For regression modeling, we then further restrict attention to the variables directly required for the analyses and to trips with complete information on all of these fields, yielding an analytic modeling dataset with 294,309 complete observations and 9 variables.

4. Methods

In this section we describe the statistical models used to address the research questions.
Our analysis proceeds in two stages. First, we fit a multiple linear regression model for the log-transformed trip duration, treating trip distance and several temporal and spatial covariates as predictors. Second, we fit a logistic regression model for the probability that a trip is long (duration greater than 10 minutes). Model assessment and diagnostics are discussed after the model specifications.

4.1 Linear regression model for log trip duration

To study factors associated with trip duration, we model the log-transformed duration as a function of trip distance, vehicle type, time of day, day of week, month, and the council district of trip origin. Let \(Y_i\) denote the trip duration in seconds for the \(i\)-th trip and let \(X_{i,\text{dist}}\) denote the trip distance. We define the continuous response \[ \log(Y_i) = \log(\text{Trip Duration}_i), \] and fit a multiple linear regression model of the form \[ \log(Y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{i,\text{dist}} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{veh}} + \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{time}} + \boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{space}} + \varepsilon_i, \] where \(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{veh}}\) collects indicator variables for vehicle type (scooter versus bicycle), \(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{time}}\) collects indicators for hour of day, day of week, and month, and \(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{space}}\) collects indicators for the council district of trip origin. The error terms \(\varepsilon_i\) are assumed to be independent with mean zero and constant variance. Under this model, \(\exp(\beta_1)\) can be interpreted as the multiplicative change in the expected trip duration associated with a one-unit increase in trip distance, holding all other covariates fixed. Coefficients for the indicator variables represent multiplicative changes relative to their respective reference categories.

In the implementation, we treat trip distance as a continuous predictor and include vehicle type, hour, day of week, month, and council district as categorical variables. Scooter is used as the reference vehicle type; reference levels for the temporal and spatial factors are determined by the default ordering in R. The model is estimated by ordinary least squares using the cleaned analytic dataset described in Section 2.1.

Day-of-week is treated as a categorical factor with Sunday as the reference category; hour-of-day uses 0:00–0:59 as the reference hour, January as the reference month, and trips starting in Council District 0 as the reference spatial category.

4.2 Logistic regression model for long trips

To address the second research question—predicting whether a trip lasts longer than ten minutes—we fit a logistic regression model using the binary indicator \[ L_i = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if trip duration exceeds 600 seconds},\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \] Let \(p_i = \Pr(L_i = 1)\) denote the probability that trip \(i\) is a long trip. We model the log-odds of a long trip as \[ \log\left(\frac{p_i}{1 - p_i}\right) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X_{i,\text{dist}} + \boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{veh}} + \boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{time}} + \boldsymbol{\psi}^{\top} \mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{space}}. \]

As before, \(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{veh}}\) contains indicator variables for vehicle type (with scooter as the reference category);
\(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{time}}\) contains indicators for hour of day, day of week, and month;
and \(\mathbf{Z}_{i,\text{space}}\) contains indicators for the council district of trip origin.

Under this model, \(\exp(\alpha_1)\) represents the multiplicative change in the odds of taking a long trip associated with a one-unit increase in trip distance.
Exponentiated coefficients for the categorical predictors correspond to odds ratios relative to their respective reference categories.

The model is estimated via maximum likelihood using the cleaned analytic dataset described in Section~2.1.
Predicted probabilities and fitted values will be examined in Section5 to assess model fit and interpret the influence of key predictors.

5.1 Linear regression results

The regression output summarized in this subsection reports the estimated coefficients from the multiple linear regression model for \(\log(\text{Trip Duration})\). The model explains a moderate portion of the variability in trip duration:

\[ R^2 = 0.2057, \qquad \text{Adjusted } R^2 = 0.2056. \]

Although trip durations vary substantially across riders and conditions, the global F-test indicates that the model as a whole is highly statistically significant (\(p < 2 \times 10^{-16}\)).

Trip distance is the strongest single predictor. The estimated coefficient is \[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{dist}} = 9.786 \times 10^{-4} \qquad (p < 2 \times 10^{-16}), \] which implies \[ \exp(9.786 \times 10^{-4}) - 1 \approx 0.098\%. \] Thus, each additional unit of distance increases the expected duration by approximately \(0.1\%\), holding all other covariates fixed.

Relative to scooters (the reference category):

Many hour-of-day and day-of-week indicators are statistically significant. Morning hours (8–12) and late-night hours (22–2) tend to have shorter expected durations (negative coefficients). Weekend indicators, especially Friday and Saturday, show meaningful positive effects: \[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{DOW\_f5}} = 0.0936, \qquad \widehat{\beta}_{\text{DOW\_f6}} = 0.1036, \] corresponding to approximately \(9\%\)–\(11\%\) longer durations relative to the reference day.

Several months differ from the reference month (January). For example: \[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{Feb}} = -0.0195, \qquad \widehat{\beta}_{\text{Mar}} = +0.0375. \] These patterns may reflect seasonal changes in travel behavior or weather.

Many council districts show significantly positive coefficients (e.g., Districts 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9), indicating longer expected durations for trips beginning in central or higher-density neighborhoods.

5.2 Logistic regression results

We next model whether a trip exceeds 10 minutes using logistic regression. The model shows strong overall significance, with a residual deviance of 134,729 on 294,263 degrees of freedom and an AIC of 134,821.

Trip distance remains the dominant predictor: \[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{dist}} = 5.99 \times 10^{-4}, \qquad p < 2 \times 10^{-16}, \] which implies \[ \exp(5.99 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 1.0006. \] Thus, each additional unit of distance increases the odds of a long trip by approximately \(0.06\%\).

Relative to scooters:

Several hour-of-day coefficients are significant. Early morning hours (5–6 AM) show much lower odds of long trips, while midday and evening periods show elevated odds, consistent with commuting or leisure patterns.

Friday and Saturday again show strong positive associations: \[ \widehat{\beta}_{\text{DOW\_f5}} = 0.298, \qquad \widehat{\beta}_{\text{DOW\_f6}} = 0.333, \] corresponding to: \[ \exp(0.298) \approx 1.35, \qquad \exp(0.333) \approx 1.40. \] Thus, the odds of a long trip are 35–40% higher on Fridays and Saturdays.

Most council district indicators are not individually significant after adjusting for distance and temporal variables, suggesting that in predicting long trips.
